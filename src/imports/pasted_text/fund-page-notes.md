 
Notes for final version
The page is sectioned, not flat. Five labelled bands — Vital signs, Performance, Risk profile, Composition, Scenarios, Sales narrative — with small section labels in pale blue between them. This gives the eye anchors during scroll and makes the page navigable by jump-link rather than purely sequentially. The jump nav at the top is small but earns its place because the page is genuinely long; users running back into a meeting need to land on the right section fast.
The reading order is sales-intent-shaped, not analyst-shaped. Vital signs answers "what's the headline number". Performance answers "how has it done and how has it drawn down". Risk profile answers "what does it do in stress". Composition answers "where does the outperformance come from". Scenarios answers "what could a client do with it". Sales narrative answers "what do I actually say". Each section is a question the salesperson would ask in sequence as they prepare for a client conversation.
Scenarios is paired. Blend on top, withdrawal stress test below. The blend constructs a what-if portfolio; the stress test runs that portfolio (or the unblended fund) through a regime. They share a mental model — they should share visual proximity. A user setting up a 70/30 blend will then naturally want to see how that blend would have done through COVID; the layout reinforces that flow.
Sales narrative is the last band but the most important. The lens selector grid sits above the generated three-column output so the user can re-pick a lens and see the prose recompute. The lens pill in the output header makes the active framing visible at all times. The action buttons (email, deck, copy, regenerate) are clustered top-right of the output box rather than scattered, so the user knows where to go to actually use the analysis.
A few design tensions worth surfacing for your team's discussion before build:
Lens scope on this page is broader than on the news page. On the news page each lens corresponds to a specific user-uploaded document or data slice (a particular sales priority, a specific market commentary). On the fund page the lenses are more abstract framings — they shape the narrative voice and evidence emphasis but not which underlying data is used (the analysis still draws from everything on the page). Worth being clear that these are different lens types: news lenses are retrieval-driven, fund lenses are framing-driven. They share a UI pattern but not their backend.
Page length is a real constraint. On a 14-inch laptop scrolling end-to-end will take maybe eight full screens. That's defensible because the page is doing real work, but it argues for the jump nav being sticky at the top during scroll, not just sitting at the top of the document. Worth thinking about either making the nav sticky or adding a small "back to top" affordance once the user is past the second section.



Notes on first version
The comparator selector in the header is the most important single change. Today's design pre-loads MSCI World as benchmark. The new version lets the user actively pick one benchmark and stack one or more competitor funds alongside it, with everything on the page recomputing. The pill-with-x pattern lets them add and remove comparators without leaving the page. This is the single most-used action in a sales analysis session.
The KPI strip is relative-first. Each tile shows the absolute number prominently, but the delta line below shows the gap to each selected comparator (benchmark and competitor). The Alpha tile gains a t-stat — when an alpha number is challenged ("is that statistically real?"), salespeople need the answer at hand. Active share replaces Beta because it's a more relevant high-conviction metric for client conversations than Beta.
Capture ratios and downside metrics get their own panel. Six small stats — upside capture, downside capture, Sortino, CVaR, percentage of down-months outperformed, average recovery time. The asymmetric capture (104/78) is the headline number for any defensive pitch. Below the stats is an auto-generated one-sentence "sales angle" that translates the numbers into a usable line.
The cumulative return chart adds a rolling outperformance ribbon (the faint green band) showing the rolling 3Y excess return over the benchmark. This is what proves consistency — "we've been ahead in every rolling window" is more compelling than "we're ahead today".
The drawdown chart compares all three (fund/benchmark/rival) on one axis and shows recovery time as a legend annotation. The recovery time is itself a sales metric — pension and retiree clients ask about it constantly.
The regime panel is new. It segments performance by VIX regime and by rate-cycle phase. It's the answer to "how does this fund behave when things get weird", which is the single hardest question salespeople face and currently have no canned answer to.
Attribution becomes a table with a Brinson decomposition and a qualitative note per row. The Brinson split (allocation effect vs selection effect) is what lets the salesperson say "94% of our outperformance is stock selection, not market timing" — the auto-generated summary in the total row makes that exact point. The plain-English explanation column is generated by the LLM from your fund data and the asset-class return data. The toggle "vs Rival X" recomputes the attribution to show why the fund beat the competitor specifically, which is a different decomposition than vs the benchmark.
Top holdings gains four columns: benchmark weight, active position, return, and contribution to performance. The little quote icon at the end of each row generates a one-line talking point about that specific holding — useful when a client zeroes in on a single name. Concentration metrics (top-10 weight, active share) appear in the header strip.
The sales narrative footer is the workflow capstone. Three columns, generated from everything above: the headline pitch, why it works (mechanistic explanation), and likely objections with bridges. Email/Deck/Copy/Regenerate buttons in the header strip. This is the section the salesperson uses to actually compose a client message — they don't have to write anything; they edit.
The copy-to-email-or-deck pattern: every panel has small snip controls in its header (camera icon for image, mail icon for email-formatted text, presentation icon for slide-ready). Hovering on a panel reveals them more prominently. Click any of them and a small modal shows a preview sized for the destination, with a "copy" button. The data structures should support two formats per panel — a clean PNG/SVG sized 1200×675 (a slide-half) and a prose paragraph with embedded numbers ready for an email body.

The blended portfolio comparison is essentially a portfolio constructor with a slider. Two funds in, a blend percentage, and the output is a third performance series that gets overlaid on the chart. The interesting bit is that the stats you can derive from just the return series are narrower than what you can derive when you have holdings — you get return, vol, Sharpe, drawdown, capture ratios, correlation, beta-to-each, but you don't get attribution or active share. So the blended panel should be honest about that and just show the return-series-derived stats cleanly.
The regime drawdown / withdrawal chart is conceptually a sequence-of-returns simulator. A lump sum of, say, £100k goes in at the start of a chosen historical regime, you draw 4% annualised (typically monthly), and you plot the residual portfolio value over time for each variant (fund, competitor, blend, benchmark). This is the sequencing risk visualisation — the killer chart for any retiree or decumulation pitch. The withdrawal rate slider lets the salesperson dial in the client's actual planned withdrawal rate. The regime presets (Iranian oil crisis, COVID, 2022 correction, GFC, dotcom) are stored periods; the user can also custom-pick a date range.
The sales lens for qualitative analysis mirrors what we built on the news page. Before the LLM generates the explanatory paragraphs, the user picks a lens — defensive positioning, vs-competitor, sequencing-risk-narrative, inflation-hedge, retirement income, ESG/sustainability, etc. The same fund data produces different prose depending on the chosen frame. This should sit above the sales narrative footer rather than inside it.
One UX decision worth flagging: blended portfolio + regime simulator are related but distinct tools. The blend is "construct a what-if portfolio"; the regime simulator is "stress-test it through a historical period". Putting them in one panel risks confusion. I'll group them as a connected pair — blend-construction on top, regime-stress on the bottom, so the blend feeds the simulator.

On the blended portfolio panel
The slider is the centre of gravity — drag and everything recomputes. The visual blend bar under the slider gives an immediate sense of the mix without reading numbers. Three series on the chart (your fund, competitor, blend) all anchored at 100 so the user reads relative outcomes naturally.
The stats matrix below is deliberately restricted to what return series alone can produce: return, volatility, Sharpe, max drawdown, capture ratios, correlation. No attribution, no active share — those need holdings data and the user said they're only uploading prices for some funds. The correlation cell is filled in only for the competitor row because that's the diversification number the salesperson actually cares about.
The bottom line is the one that sells the blend: "92% of the return with lower vol than either fund alone". A blend isn't a head-to-head pitch — it's a complementary pitch, useful for clients who already own the competitor and won't sell out wholesale. The auto-generated paragraph names that frame explicitly.
A few mechanics worth flagging: the blend assumes monthly rebalancing which is the convention but worth being explicit about (noted under the slider) — quarterly or annual rebalancing produces meaningfully different results. Worth letting the user toggle this later. Also, when the user adds more than one competitor at the page level, the blend control should support more than two legs — a 50/30/20 across three funds — but the v1 two-leg version is the right place to start.
On the regime / withdrawal simulator
Regimes appear as preset pills first, with a "Custom" option using a date-range picker at the end. The presets carry the periods most likely to be asked about: COVID, 2022 stocks-and-bonds, Iran oil, GFC, dotcom. You'd back this with a stored library that you can grow over time.
The chart shows residual portfolio value over the regime, with the dashed line at £95k visually marking the danger zone where drawdowns + withdrawals compound. Three series — fund, competitor, blend — anchored at £100k. The end-value annotations on the right edge let the eye land directly on the punchline.
The four stat tiles below decompose what matters in a sequencing-risk argument: where you ended up, how bad it got at the worst point, how long until you were back to break-even, and what you actually withdrew over the period. The "worst-point value" stat is the killer for a retiree pitch because it answers "how scared would I have been?" — not just "what's my final number?"
The withdrawal-rate slider lets the salesperson dial in the client's actual planned rate. 4% is the convention but a 6% withdrawal makes the gap between funds much more dramatic, and a 3% withdrawal narrows it. Letting the user move this in the meeting itself ("what if you draw 5%?") is the kind of moment that closes business.
A note on the math: this is historical sequence-of-returns. It's not a Monte Carlo — you're not simulating, you're replaying what actually happened. That's the right choice for a sales tool because clients trust "this is what would have happened to your money" far more than "this is what our simulation suggests". Worth being explicit about this in the panel footer or tooltip.
On the sales lens for qualitative analysis
Six lenses, structured the same way as the news-page lenses for consistency: a category meta-label, a one-line title, a one-line description of what the resulting analysis will emphasise. The currently selected lens gets the blue border treatment.
The lens determines the frame of the generated paragraphs, not the underlying data. The same fund delivers six genuinely different stories: defensive positioning emphasises capture and recovery; sequencing risk emphasises withdrawal outcomes; head-to-head emphasises attribution; conviction emphasises selection vs allocation; diversification emphasises blend stats; thematic emphasises sleeves like commodities or AI capex.
Below the lens grid is the generated qualitative analysis, with the same three-column structure used elsewhere (headline pitch / supporting evidence / likely objections). The active lens is shown as a pill at the top so the salesperson can see at a glance what frame they're working in. Email, deck, copy, regenerate live where users will look for them.
Two things to think about for the lens architecture:
Lens libraries should be editable by the user, not hard-coded by you. Salespeople have their own framings and your six options will quickly feel limiting. The right architecture is to ship six defaults but let users add and save custom lenses — name, description, and a stored prompt template — that get attached to their account. This also makes the product stickier because users invest in tuning it.
Lens scope matters. The news-page lens is "match this headline to a relevant frame". The fund-page lens is "frame this analysis for a specific conversation type". They feel similar but they're doing different work — the news lens is a retrieval problem (find which user document is most relevant), the fund lens is a framing problem (compose the same data differently). The UI looks the same; the backend logic doesn't. Worth being clear with your team about this distinction so the implementations don't get confused
